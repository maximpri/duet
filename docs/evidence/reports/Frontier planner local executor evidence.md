# Frontier planning could not rescue local execution

Duet missed its goal because it gave the weaker model the seat where both published research and duet's own experiments locate a coding agent's quality: the multi-turn loop that writes, runs and repairs code. Duet then wrapped that model in a phase-machine harness that cost more points than frontier planning and review could win back. Its best blind score was **39/50 at 95% local routing**. In the same judging the frontier-only reference scored **48**, and the same local model running in a plain single-loop agent (Pi) scored **41**. In a controlled test the same local model scored **43 in Pi and 18 in duet**, and the ≥80% local share was reached only in that one run. Published studies predict this pattern. Strong planners paired with weaker tool-calling executors do worse than the strong model working alone. Multi-agent coordination slightly lowered SWE-bench Verified scores in every architecture tested. Under the same harness, models small enough to run locally trail the frontier by roughly 20–50 points on SWE-bench Verified, and the gap widens on longer tasks. The cost case was weaker than assumed. 92% of frontier spend went to reviewing diffs, a fixed coordination cost that moving work to the local model does not shrink. Total cost was never measured. Where cloud spend was estimated, duet saved 9.65% at 2× the wall time, or 71.3% at 3×, in both cases against a cheap GLM Flash baseline. Meanwhile, prompt processing before the first output token (prefill) took 64% of the local model's time. The ±1-point bar was also narrower than the judge's own 40–48 spread on an unchanged file, so parity could never have been confirmed. Duet's 9-point shortfall is larger than that noise, so the failure is real. No published study tests duet's exact setup, and every documented hybrid that works keeps a strong model writing the code.

## Quality is decided in the execution seat duet gave away

No study tests duet's exact configuration: a frontier planner and reviewer, with a ≤32B local model running every tool-calling turn on a coding task. The adjacent evidence nonetheless agrees on one point. An agent's quality depends mainly on the model that makes the execution turns, and a stronger planner does not buy that quality back.

### Strong planners do not rescue weak executors

The closest controlled comparison is D-CIPHER, a planner/executor agent for capture-the-flag security tasks. With the planner held strong, weakening the tool-calling executor produced "consistent under-performance". On NYU CTF Bench, Claude 3.5 Sonnet in both roles solved **19.0%**, Sonnet planning for a Haiku executor solved **13.0%**, GPT-4o planning for GPT-4o-mini solved 6.5%, and Llama 405B planning for Llama 70B solved nothing. The authors conclude that both roles "require stronger models" ([arXiv 2502.10931](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.10931)). The results usually cited in favour of planning come from setups unlike duet's. Plan-and-Act's claim that "the bottleneck may lie more in plan quality" used the same fine-tuned Llama-70B in both roles on single-click web actions. Its biggest gain, from 43.6% to 53.9% on WebArena-Lite, came from regenerating the plan after every executor action ([arXiv 2503.09572](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09572)). Strong-to-weak plan injection for CUDA kernels did help weaker models, but "planning without feedback degrades performance" ([arXiv 2605.26720](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.26720)). Duet's frontier model planned once at the start and saw execution only through the local model's trajectory.

Two 2026 studies explain why the plan cannot carry the task. ORACLE-SWE gave SWE-agent perfect versions of five signals. A reproduction test raised GPT-4o on SWE-bench Verified from 39.4% to **75.5% (+36.1 points)**. Knowing the edit location, the kind of thing a planner can supply up front, added only 13.2 points ([arXiv 2604.07789](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07789)). The most valuable information is produced and used during execution, and in duet execution belonged to the local model. Review also cannot add work the executor never produces. In the boosting study, eight GPT-5.4 nano proposals with critic selection raised SWE-bench Verified from 67.0% to 76.4%. Even an oracle picking the best of eight reached only 79.0%, because "remaining failures are mostly proposal-coverage failures … that stronger selection alone cannot close" ([arXiv 2605.14163](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14163)). Duet's correct-but-plain game is an instance of this ceiling. A frontier reviewer can reject defects, but it cannot conjure the seven levels, pause screen and fireworks the local author never wrote. Duet's own untested "authorship" hypothesis holds that the 45+ tier needs frontier-written code, and this research predicts exactly that ([HANDOFF-GOAL.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/HANDOFF-GOAL.md)).

### Every split that worked kept a strong model writing

Aider's architect/editor results are often cited in support of duet's split, yet they point the other way. Its best pairs used strong editors: o1-preview directing o1-mini or DeepSeek scored 85.0%, against 79.7% for o1-preview alone ([aider](https://aider.chat/2024/09/26/architect.html)). The January 2025 polyglot record paired R1 as architect with Claude 3.5 Sonnet as **editor** and scored 64.0% for $13.29, "14X less cost" than the previous o1 record. The savings came from a cheap architect, not a cheap editor ([aider](https://aider.chat/2025/01/24/r1-sonnet.html)). Weaker editors lowered pair scores: Sonnet directing DeepSeek scored 78.9% against 80.5% for Sonnet with itself, and Aider found that other editors "didn't seem to improve o1 or R1 versus their solo scores". The only all-local pair, QwQ directing Qwen2.5-Coder-32B, reached 73.6% against 71.4% for the editor alone ([aider](https://aider.chat/2024/12/03/qwq.html)). Aider's editor also has a narrower job than duet's executor. It makes one pass that turns a written change description into edits, with no search, no test runs and no state carried across turns.

Practitioners had already dropped the pattern duet brought back. Cognition described 2024 systems in which a large model explained changes and a small model applied them: "the small model would misinterpret the instructions of the large model and make an incorrect edit due to the most slight ambiguities." Deciding and applying the edit went back to a single model ([Cognition 2025](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)). In an April 2026 follow-up, Cognition called a weaker primary model that consults a stronger one "still an open problem, and we think it's a training one." Its SWE 1.5 model was "not good enough at being the primary model", because the gap to Sonnet 4.5 "was too wide in exactly the places that mattered … knowing when to escalate, knowing what to ask" ([Cognition 2026](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)).

The large savings in the routing literature come from routing independent queries one at a time. RouteLLM cut cost by over 85% on MT-Bench at 95% of GPT-4 quality, but only 35% on GSM8K ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)), and FrugalGPT reported up to 98% ([arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)). The two 2026 routers that route inside multi-turn episodes restrict the weak model sharply. SWE-Router lets the weak model run only the first four exploration turns on SWE-bench Verified. When it escalates, the strong model **restarts from scratch**, because "conditioning m2 on m1's reasoning has been seen to bias m2 toward m1's mistakes" ([arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00053)). MTRouter beat GPT-5 alone on ScienceWorld (48.4 → 53.8) at 58.7% lower cost, but its pool held only near-frontier models and it switched models about five times per episode rather than twenty ([arXiv 2604.23530](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23530)). Stanford's Minions is the strongest local–remote result. It kept **97.9% of GPT-4o quality at 18% of the cloud cost**, but only after the local model's work was restructured into single-step, short-context extraction jobs run in parallel. The authors name the small model's weaknesses as multi-step instructions (splitting them apart was worth 56 points) and long contexts (a 13% drop). They tested no coding or tool-use tasks ([arXiv 2502.15964](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15964)). A coding executor's job is exactly multi-step, long-context and stateful.

### Local-class models trail most on long multi-turn work

The capability gap is largest on the work duet handed to the local model. Under the SWE-bench team's identical mini-SWE-agent harness, Claude 4.5 Opus resolves 76.8% of SWE-bench Verified, Devstral Small (24B) 56.4%, gpt-oss-120b 26.0% and Qwen2.5-Coder-32B 9.0%. The local-class models also make 2–3× more calls per task: 86.9 for Devstral against 28.9 for Opus 4.6 ([SWE-bench leaderboard data](https://github.com/SWE-bench/swe-bench.github.io/blob/master/data/leaderboards.json)). Vendors report scores 12–36 points above these same-harness runs; gpt-oss-120b's model card claims 62.4% ([arXiv 2508.10925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10925)). In Qwen's own comparison table the harness alone moved Kimi-K2.5 from 49.4 to 0.9 on Terminal-Bench 2.0. The same report shows the gap growing with task length: Qwen3-Coder-Next trails Opus 4.5 by about 7 points on SWE-bench Verified but by 21–24 on Terminal-Bench 2.0 ([Qwen3-Coder-Next report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00729v1)). METR measures the length of task a model completes half the time, its "time horizon". It put gpt-oss-120b at 45 minutes and GPT-5, measured the same week, at 131 ([METR](https://metr.org/assets/benchmark_results_1_0.yaml)).

The skills that fail are the agentic ones. On SWE-Bench Pro, Qwen3 32B has the highest tool-error rate, at 42.0% of failed runs. Under a 50-turn, $2 cap it resolved 3.4% of tasks against GPT-5's 23.3% ([arXiv 2509.16941](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16941v2)). Epoch estimates that open-weight models trail closed frontier models by about four months overall ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-eci-gap)), and that models fitting a single consumer GPU trail by 6–12 months ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/consumer-gpu-model-gap)). Newer 2026 local models look much stronger on vendor cards; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B claims 73.4% on SWE-bench Verified ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B)). No independent same-harness run confirms this, and duet's own local model is recorded only as a Qwen variant behind an alias.

## The harness subtracted more points than the frontier added

With the model held fixed, the choice of harness usually moves coding-benchmark scores by 3–17 points. On Terminal-Bench 2.0, Claude Opus 4.5 scored 57.8% in the one-tool Terminus 2 harness and 52.1% in Claude Code, which used **66× more input tokens** (256.9M vs 3.9M). Haiku 4.5 ranged from 29.8% down to 13.3% across four harnesses, with the low score in OpenHands after 663M input tokens ([Terminal-Bench 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11868)). Epoch found that scaffold choice had "the single biggest impact", up to 15% for Kimi K2 Thinking ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/why-benchmarking-is-hard)). Swings get much larger when a harness mechanically blocks the model. Changing only the edit tool took Grok Code Fast 1 from 6.7% to 68.3%, and the weakest models gained the most ([Can Bölük](https://stencil.so/blog/the-harness-problem)). Duet lost 25 of 50 points with the same model, the equivalent of 50 points out of 100. That places it in the blocked-model regime, and weaker models like duet's executor are the most sensitive to the harness.

Controlled multi-agent studies predicted a loss, not a gain. Google's scaling study covered 260 configurations. Every multi-agent architecture lowered SWE-bench Verified by 2–15% compared with a single agent given the same compute. The study also fitted a ceiling: once a single agent scores above about 45%, adding agents gives negative returns ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). Pi's local-only run scored 80–86% of duet's rubric, far above that ceiling. A 2026 matched comparison found that at most one of six multi-agent systems beat a single-agent baseline. The other five trailed by 2.6–11.3 points and cost more ([arXiv 2606.05670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05670)). The MAST taxonomy, built from 1,642 multi-agent traces, attributes most failures to system design and coordination. Its largest failure modes are step repetition (15.7%), a mismatch between stated reasoning and the action taken (13.2%) and not recognising when to stop (12.4%). With Qwen2.5-Coder-32B inside the ChatDev framework, 96 of 100 traces showed step repetition and 94 showed stopping failures. The best redesign using the same model added only 15.6 points ([arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). Duet showed the same signatures. A brick-hit code block was written out three times across passes that kept no memory of each other. Executors re-read a 60 KB artifact they had already seen, and planners produced 4.6 MB of reasoning without ever submitting a plan ([HANDOFF-GOAL.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/HANDOFF-GOAL.md); [PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md); [GOAL_STATUS.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/docs/GOAL_STATUS.md)).

Duet's pass boundaries broke continuous work into repeated handoffs, which research on context penalizes. Each pass kept only the host's receipts, with no conversation history, tool results or reasoning. The next pass started by rebuilding a 10–30K-token opening context, and one goal needed about 94 manual resumes ([REVIEW_2026-08-27.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/REVIEW_2026-08-27.md); [long-horizon-plan-2026-08-20.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/long-horizon-plan-2026-08-20.md)). Across 15 LLMs, spreading a task's information over several turns cut performance by 39% on average and more than doubled unreliability, and recap-style fixes recovered only 15–20% of the loss ([arXiv 2505.06120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). JetBrains found that LLM summaries made SWE-agent runs about 15% longer by hiding failure signals. Simply masking old observations inside one continuous loop matched the summaries' solve rate at half the cost ([arXiv 2508.21433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21433)). Cognition's first principle is to "share full agent traces, not just individual messages" ([Cognition 2025](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)). Duet briefs the executor and rebuilds its context every pass, so it breaks that rule by construction.

Duet's own reviews reached the same verdict. The 08-24 host review counted 216 refusal sites where "host judgment" sat between the model and reality. It found that "every point duet has ever gained came from removing host friction, not adding host intelligence." Removing hard turn caps took the score from 16 to 25, and letting the executor look at its own work took it to 30. Six behavioural checks written by the host all failed validation and were reverted ([host-review-2026-08-24.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/host-review-2026-08-24.md)). The unit of work was a pass that could end on any of 22 host limits. Every mechanism that raised quality shipped switched off by default, so "a fresh install ships the ~20/50 configuration" ([REVIEW_2026-08-27.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/REVIEW_2026-08-27.md)). Anthropic's rule for harnesses applies directly: "Every component in a harness encodes an assumption about what the model can't do on its own," and each such assumption should be stress-tested and removed once it goes stale ([Anthropic, harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). The project's own pre-registered falsification tests failed twice. Defragmentation was supposed to lift the score above 40 or show that "the split model is not recoverable", and it scored 24. The host review's top three fixes were all implemented and then scored 39 and 38.5 against a bar of 40, an outcome the review said should lead the project to "question the phase machine itself." The 08-27 review then recommended re-founding the executor as a single conversation with a stable prompt prefix. The September architecture kept the split anyway ([HANDOFF-GOAL.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/HANDOFF-GOAL.md); [DELIVERY_PLAN.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/docs/DELIVERY_PLAN.md)).

Duet spent most of its frontier budget on verification, and the evidence shows which kinds pay. Verification helps when it brings in new ground truth: running tests, a checklist the agent completes before declaring itself done, or parallel attempts filtered by regression tests. LangChain's single-agent harness changes, which included such a checklist, added 13.7 points on Terminal-Bench 2.0 ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/improving-deep-agents-with-harness-engineering)). Parallel attempts with test-based rejection took Claude 3.7 Sonnet from 63.7% to 70.3% on SWE-bench Verified ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-3-7-sonnet)). An LLM opinion that adds no new signal lengthens runs and can be too lenient. Anthropic's evaluator would "identify legitimate issues, then talk itself into deciding they weren't a big deal and approve the work anyway" ([Anthropic, harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Duet's frontier judged "a checklist over a diff", never the spec or the running program. When its adversarial review round overran a ~350-second deadline, the accepted verdict was thrown away and every criterion was marked unmet, which happened nine times on 08-26 alone ([REVIEW_2026-08-27.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/REVIEW_2026-08-27.md); [HANDOFF-GOAL.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/HANDOFF-GOAL.md)). The split did achieve something. Duet's verification machinery produced the best correctness score of the three agents on 08-25, and Anthropic's own planner-generator-evaluator harness turned a broken solo game into a working app. But Anthropic used frontier models in every role, spent $200 and six hours against $9 and 20 minutes for the solo run, and simplified the harness once the model improved. Verification can raise a strong author's floor, but it could not raise a weak author's ceiling.

## Offloading tokens saved little money and cost hours

The economic case assumed that frontier spend grows with execution tokens, so moving execution local would cut it. It did not work that way, because supervising a weaker executor is itself frontier work. On a small task on 09-07, duet as a hybrid was compared with duet running GLM-5.3-Flash in every role, and both passed 16/16. The hybrid's cloud cost came to 90.35% of the control's entire cost, a **9.65% saving at 2.05× the wall time**. The frontier's final audit alone was 42.73% of the hybrid's cloud spend, and the verdict was that "Frontier coordination dominates the remaining cost" ([MATCHED_DOGFOOD_COMPARISON_2026-09-07.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/docs/MATCHED_DOGFOOD_COMPARISON_2026-09-07.md)). On a larger 73-check task on 09-08, duet used **71.3% less cloud cost at API-equivalent prices than Pi running GLM-5.3-Flash, but took 2.98× as long**. In absolute terms that was $0.027 against $0.094, because the baseline was a cheap model and 96% of its cloud input was served from cache ([ROADMAP_MATCHED_REFERENCE_2026-09-08.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/docs/ROADMAP_MATCHED_REFERENCE_2026-09-08.md)). The Captain Comic run spent 259K tokens on review and adversarial review and ended 94% frontier ([CAPTAIN_COMIC_HOST_REVIEW_2026-09-16.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/docs/CAPTAIN_COMIC_HOST_REVIEW_2026-09-16.md)). Anthropic measures multi-agent systems at about 15× the tokens of chat ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Delegation raises total tokens and saves money only if the frontier's own context stays small.

Local tokens were cheap in dollars but expensive in time. On one Arkanoid repair drive, **249 of 387 local-model minutes (64%) passed before the first output token**. 65% of the 5.0M input tokens came from cache, yet each action turn still added about 7,000 new tokens as the executor re-read an artifact it had already seen. Uncached prefill ran at about 117 tokens per second, which meant a median 42.8 seconds to the first token and a worst case of 443 seconds ([PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md)). That rate is low even for a local machine. llama.cpp prefills gpt-oss-20b (3.6B active parameters) on an M4 Max at 1,094–1,850 tokens per second across 2K–32K contexts ([llama.cpp #16578](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/16578)). A dense 32B model on an M3 Ultra manages about 271 tokens per second at 32K ([MLX #3209](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/discussions/3209)). In the cloud, Claude Sonnet 5 adds about 0.13 seconds of latency per extra 10,000 cached-context tokens ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/long-context-latency-scaling-gpt-vs-claude)). Local caching is also fragile. llama.cpp and MLX servers reuse only a byte-identical prompt prefix, and on 2026 hybrid-attention models such as Qwen3.5/3.6 they often fell back to reprocessing the whole prompt. One user reported a 15K-token conversation taking about eight minutes per turn ([llama.cpp #20225](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/20225); [mlx-lm #980](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/issues/980)). Because local models make 2–3× more calls per task, this per-turn cost multiplies.

In dollars, local execution competes with cheap cloud models rather than with the frontier. At duet's measured 117 tokens per second, a million prefilled tokens takes 2.4 hours. At the M5 Max's 200 W maximum draw and the 18.2¢/kWh US average, that costs about $0.09 of electricity (calculated from [Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102027) and [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/report/elec_coal_renew.php)). The table sets that against the alternatives.

With the frontier on a flat-rate plan, as in duet's actual setup, moving tokens local saves nothing until a usage cap binds. After that the saving is at most the price step to the next tier, such as $100 a month between Claude Max 5x and 20x ([Claude Max](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11049741-what-is-the-max-plan)). The Minions authors named the same blind spot in their own work: they treated local compute as free, ignoring hardware and energy ([arXiv 2502.15964](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15964)). Duet's 1.9–4.8M tokens per run are not unusual either. Dividing the Terminal-Bench 4.0 leaderboard's per-entry totals by its 330 trials gives 4.6–26M tokens per trial for frontier agents ([tbench.ai](https://www.tbench.ai/leaderboard/terminal-bench/2.0)).

## A ±1 bar sat below the judge's own noise

The quality bar could not have confirmed success even if duet had reached it. The unchanged GLM reference file scored 40, 41, 42, 43, 43, 48 and 47.5 across judgings, and the local reference scored 38–42.5. The 08-27 review put judge noise at roughly seven times the ±1 bar. It found no repeat judgings and no recorded seeds. The artifacts' `<title>` tags identified each one to the judge. The judging prompt anchored the judge with "standing frontier-only baseline scores 45–48/50", and the codex judge defaulted to the lane's own model ([REVIEW_2026-08-27.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/REVIEW_2026-08-27.md)). The comparison was loose in other ways too. The "frontier reference" was three different agents, and duet's routed lane was never judged against duet's own frontier-only configuration on this task. The product carried four conflicting definitions of success, ranging from "within 5 points and 4× fewer frontier tokens" to the operator's ±1 at ≥80% local ([REVIEW_2026-09-22.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/REVIEW_2026-09-22.md)).

Research on LLM judges explains the noise. Strong judges agree with humans about as often as humans agree with each other, 85% versus 81% on non-tied MT-Bench pairs. But GPT-4 kept its verdict only 65% of the time when the two answers were swapped, and it favoured its own outputs by about 10 points of win rate, Claude-v1 by 25 ([arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). Qwen-3 as a judge gave the same MT-Bench verdict on all three repeat runs for only 61.3% of items ([arXiv 2510.27106](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27106v1)). Agentic results vary between runs as well. Single-run SWE-bench Verified pass rates swing 2.2–6.0 points depending on which run is picked, even at temperature zero ([arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07150)), and Anthropic advises skepticism toward differences under 3 points ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise)). Miller's standard power formula shows the scale of the problem ([arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)). Suppose a judge's repeat range implies a standard deviation near 3 points. Detecting a 1-point difference then needs roughly 140 judgments per configuration, and a formal equivalence test needs about 150. With one artifact per configuration, the variation between runs of the agent itself cannot be estimated at all, however many times each artifact is re-judged.

The noise undermines only a claim of success. Duet's best score of 39 is below every frontier-reference score on record (40–48), and duet trailed the local-only agent in every three-way judging, so the negative result holds despite the instrument's error. What the noise destroyed was the project's ability to steer. Single-run A/B tests drove design decisions that later reversed. The diagnosed cause of slow prefill moved from cache-busting to re-reads once it was actually measured. The judge's claimed "±1" stability turned out to be about 7 points, and a feature flag that failed on its own helped in combination. The project's notes concede that "solo A/B on a stochastic pipeline at n=1 is weaker evidence than I treated it as" ([findings-2026-08-22.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/findings-2026-08-22.md); [PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md](/Users/maximp/OpenCode/duet_agent/notes/PREFILL_MEASUREMENT_2026-08-28.md)). All of the quality evidence also comes from a single task type, a single-file browser game.

## Documented hybrids keep the strong model on the keyboard

Every major coding host now lets subagents use their own model, and OpenCode and Codex CLI can point at a local Ollama or LM Studio server ([OpenCode](https://opencode.ai/docs/providers/); [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/config-file/config-advanced)). The documented patterns differ from duet in one decisive respect: which model writes the code.

| Pattern | Who writes code | Frontier's role | Best published evidence | Fit for ≥80% local |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frontier main loop with cheap subagents | Frontier | Makes every decision and edit; subagents search and summarize in isolated contexts | Vendor docs only; no independent coding numbers ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/subagents)) | Low: most turns stay frontier |
| Advisor | Cheap but capable executor (Sonnet, Haiku) | Consulted mid-task on the shared context, 400–700 tokens per call | Sonnet + Opus advisor: +2.7 points on SWE-bench Multilingual at 11.9% lower cost. Haiku + Opus: 19.7% → 41.2% on BrowseComp ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy)) | High token share; untested with a local executor |
| Cheap architect, strong editor | Strong editor | Optional | R1 + Sonnet: 64.0% on polyglot for $13.29, 14× cheaper than o1 ([aider](https://aider.chat/2025/01/24/r1-sonnet.html)) | Wrong direction for local offload |
| Fast-apply merge model | Frontier decides; small model merges the edit | Authors every change | 96–98% merge accuracy at ~10K tok/s, vendor-reported ([Morph](https://www.morphllm.com/fast-apply-model)); untuned models fail ~10% ([Relace](https://relace.ai/blog/relace-apply-3)) | Low: mechanical step only |
| MinionS decomposition | Local model runs single-step chunk jobs | Plans and aggregates every round | 97.9% of GPT-4o quality at 18% of cloud cost, on long-document QA ([arXiv 2502.15964](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15964)) | High, but read-only QA, not coding |
| Weak-first routing with restart | Weak model for ≤4 exploration turns; strong model restarts on escalation | Solves escalated tasks from scratch | Routing quality (Route-AUC) +12 to +15 points on SWE-bench Verified ([arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00053)) | Moderate |
| Minimal single loop | One model | None | Terminus 2 beat Claude Code for Opus 4.5 with 66× fewer input tokens ([Terminal-Bench 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11868)); Pi ran duet's local model to 41–45 | Capped at local-only quality |

The table's patterns share two features. When a cheap model runs every turn, it is already a strong model. Sonnet alone scores 72.1% on SWE-bench Multilingual ([MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/anthropic-advisor-strategy-opus-sonnet-haiku)), and Haiku 4.5 scores 73.3% on SWE-bench Verified ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-haiku-4-5)). The frontier model steps in on demand with the executor's full context, rather than acting once at plan time. Even so, Haiku with an Opus advisor scored 29% below Sonnet alone on BrowseComp while costing 85% less, and the primary source is unclear about which baseline the 11.9% cost saving is measured against. When a small model touches code, it either applies edits a strong model has already decided or answers bounded questions. Claude Code's docs describe a subagent's job as absorbing "search results, logs, or file contents you won't reference again" and returning only a summary ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). No vendor documents a cheap or local model running the main loop while a frontier model only plans. OpenCode's own configuration example, a faster model for planning and a more capable one for implementation, is duet's split reversed ([OpenCode](https://opencode.ai/docs/agents/)).

For a goal that requires ≥80% local, two designs fit the evidence, and neither has been tested with a local executor. The first inverts duet's control flow. It runs the local model in a Pi-like single loop, which already reached 41–43 on duet's own rubric, and lets that model consult a frontier advisor at decision points. Frontier tokens then go to an ambitious design brief and to judging the running game, not to reading diffs. The risk is the one Cognition names: "how does a dumber model know it's at its limits?" The second keeps a frontier main loop and pushes exploration, test runs and log reading to local subagents. This matches documented practice, but duet's own measurements suggest the local share would land well below 80%, since HumanEval averaged only 30% local. Either way, an open-weight model in the cloud can fill the cheap-execution slot for cents per million tokens at cloud prefill speed, which removes hours of wall-clock time without changing the architecture. Both designs also benefit from the harness practices with measured payoffs: an append-only prompt prefix that stays byte-identical between turns, masking old observations instead of summarizing and restarting, and verification that runs the program rather than reading the diff.

## Conclusion

The goal combined three conditions that the evidence says trade against each other. Frontier-level quality comes from whichever model makes the execution turns. A ≥80% local share requires the local model to make them. Supervising a weaker executor is frontier work whose cost is fixed rather than proportional, so it erodes "a fraction of the cost" most when the executor is weakest. Duet's data trace all three. At 95% local it achieved correctness but not ambition, and when the frontier took on more of the work the local share fell to 50% or lower. The frontier runs on a flat-rate plan, and models of the local class are available in the cloud for $0.07–0.15 per million input tokens. Given both facts, the defensible benefits of local execution are privacy, offline operation and independence from providers, not price. Any cost claim should be stated in dollars actually paid and hours of wall clock, not in token share.

The deeper lesson concerns the order of work. Duet built about 300,000 lines of mechanism before it had an instrument that could detect the effect it was chasing, and its pre-registered falsification tests failed without changing the architecture. The literature's most robust findings could each have been tested cheaply against the Pi baseline that already existed: minimal loops beat heavy harnesses, coordination hurts sequential coding once a single agent scores above about 45%, and strong planners do not rescue weak executors. If the project continues, the decisive experiment is small. It would take the 41–43 local single loop, add a frontier advisor and a frontier-written design brief, and measure whether that closes the 5–7 points to the frontier reference across at least five artifacts per arm, with paired judging that swaps answer order. On coding, the only published advisor gain is +2.7 points, for an executor that was already strong. The honest expectation is therefore that frontier parity at ≥80% local is out of reach for today's local-class models, and that the project must choose between relaxing the local share and accepting local-baseline quality.
